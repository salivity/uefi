Why Intel Handed EFI to the UEFI Forum

The Extensible Firmware Interface (EFI) was handed over to the Unified EFI Forum to transform a proprietary Intel initiative into an industry-wide standard. Originally developed by Intel in the late 1990s to overcome legacy BIOS limitations for its 64-bit Itanium servers, the technology needed broader ecosystem support to succeed across consumer and enterprise hardware. By transferring the specification to an independent, multi-company consortium in 2005, Intel alleviated competitor concerns over proprietary control, distributed the burden of development, and paved the way for modern Unified Extensible Firmware Interface (UEFI) adoption across diverse computing architectures.

Intel originally created EFI under the name "Intel Boot Initiative" because the legacy PC BIOS, dating back to 1981, was ill-equipped for modern hardware. Legacy BIOS was limited to 16-bit processor mode, 1 MB of addressable memory, and archaic Master Boot Record (MBR) partitioning schemes capped at 2.2 TB. While EFI addressed these issues with modular C code, modern driver support, and the GUID Partition Table (GPT), it was tightly coupled with Intel's brand and architecture.

The primary obstacle to EFI's universal adoption was industry reluctance to accept a core firmware standard governed by a single semiconductor manufacturer. Direct competitors like AMD, original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) like HP, Dell, and IBM, and operating system vendors like Microsoft were wary of becoming dependent on Intel-controlled platform specifications. Broad market penetration required shared governance and mutual intellectual property protections that a single-vendor specification could not provide.

In 2005, Intel released the EFI version 1.10 specification to a newly formed, non-profit trade organization: the Unified EFI Forum. The founding membership included Intel alongside rival chipmakers, major PC OEMs, operating system developers, and independent BIOS vendors such as American Megatrends (AMI), Phoenix Technologies, and Insyde Software.

Relinquishing sole ownership solved the adoption bottleneck. Under the UEFI Forum, the specification was renamed UEFI, and development evolved through collaborative working groups. This neutral stewardship allowed the platform to expand rapidly beyond its original server focus, gaining native support for x86-64, ARM, enhanced networking capabilities, cryptography, and Secure Boot across almost all personal computing devices.
